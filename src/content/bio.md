I'm a Master's student in Computer Science at Dartmouth, advised by [Soroush Vosoughi](https://soroushvosoughi.com/) and mentored by [Nikhil Singh](https://nsingh1.host.dartmouth.edu/). I work on technical AI safety for multi-agent LLM systems, with a focus on the failure modes that only show up under interaction — between agents, between agents and adversaries, and between systems and the people they're deployed for.

The through-line in most of what I do is that LLM-based systems behave very differently in clean evaluation than in the regimes where they actually get used: pressured, deceived, embedded in long-horizon loops, coordinating with other agents whose objectives may not align with theirs. The auditing tools we have today were built for the clean case. I try to build the ones we'll need for the messy one. Current work lives on [research](/research) and [projects](/projects).

I came to this from an applied path. Undergrad at [NYU Shanghai](https://shanghai.nyu.edu/) and the [Courant Institute](https://cs.nyu.edu/), with [Hongyi Wen](https://whongyi.github.io/) on multimodal personalization and [Jean Ponce](https://www.di.ens.fr/~ponce/) on vision. Outside academia I've shipped production ML — recommendation engines and inference infrastructure for Fortune 500 companies, technical lead work for VC-backed startups in the [Gobi Partners](https://www.gobi.vc/) portfolio. That background is part of why "safety in deployment" sits as its own research thread for me rather than a footnote: a lot of what fails in real systems isn't a failure of the model, it's a failure of the seams between the model and everything around it.

Outside of research I'm most often in the [mountains](/mountains). There's a kind of long-horizon decision-making under uncertainty that you only really learn in places where being wrong costs something other than reviewer comments. I read philosophy and poetry too — not as counterweight to the technical work but as part of it. The failures I care about most are failures of language and meaning under pressure, and the people who've written best about those didn't call it alignment.
